## Brain

The brain is the center of the human experience. It dictates everything from our survival instincts to our deepest philosophical thoughts.
- The Triune Model: To simplify how we function, think of the brain in three layers:
    -  The reptilian brain controls survival (breathing, heart rate). 
    - The limbic system manages emotions and memory. 
    - The neocortex handles logic, language, and abstract thought.
- Cognitive Biases: Our brains use mental shortcuts (heuristics) to save energy. This often results in systematic errors in thinking, like confirmation bias (only noticing things that prove you right).

## Adult development

Robert keegan's theory of adult development:
- Stage 1: Impulsive Mind (Early Childhood): Driven by impulses and perceptions.
- Stage 2: Imperial Mind (Adolescence/Some Adults, ~6%): Ruled by personal needs,
wishes, and interests; and driven by "self-sovereign" perspectives.
- Stage 3: Socialised Mind (~58% of adults): wants to be part of the tribe. Able to
think abstractly and of others.
- Stage 4: Self-Authoring Mind (~35% of adults): Individuals can step back from social
expectations to create their own internal authority, values, and sense of identity.
- Stage 5: Self-Transforming Mind (~1% of adults): Individuals can hold multiple,
conflicting systems of meaning, recognizing the limitations of their own perspectives

## Anthropology

Economics assumes humans act rationally; sociology proves we rarely do - group dynamics, class structures, media consumption, and cognitive biases influence mass human behaviour and societal trends.

Anthropology looks at humans at a macro level, studying how biology and culture interact over millennia.
- Evolutionary Roots: For 99% of human history, we lived in small, egalitarian hunter-gatherer bands. Our biology, dietary needs, and social anxieties (like the deep fear of social rejection) are still hardwired for that lifestyle.
- Culture as an Adaptation: While other animals adapt to environments using their bodies (like growing thicker fur), humans use culture (building shelters, making clothes, creating laws). Culture is our primary survival strategy.

Anthropology - To understand global conflicts and alliances, you must understand what people value. Studying major world religions, cultural frameworks, and belief systems explains the underlying worldviews of different populations.

This creates intense loyalty to sports teams, political parties, or nations, often leading to automatic hostility toward outsiders.Class Structures: Every society stratifies people by wealth, education, or family lineage. Your social class largely dictates your access to power, your healthcare, your career path, and even how you view your own potential. It is the invisible ladder (or barrier) in daily life.Media 

- What People Value: Western societies heavily value individual liberty, personal achievement, and freedom of speech. 
- Many Eastern or Indigenous cultures place a much higher value on collective harmony, family duty, and respect for authority. 
Global conflicts often happen because one side tries to force its values onto a culture that operates on a completely different moral foundation.

Major World Religions: Religion is not just about personal faith; it is a foundational blueprint for law, morality, and identity. It dictates what millions of people consider clean or unclean, sacred or disposable, and right or wrong. You cannot understand Middle Eastern geopolitics without understanding Islam, nor can you understand US political history without understanding Christian currents.Cultural Frameworks & Belief Systems: This is the lens through which a population views time, progress, and justice. For instance, some cultures view time as linear (always moving forward toward progress), while others view it as cyclical (history repeating itself). Some cultures value face-saving and honor above all else, meaning a public insult can spark a conflict that logic cannot easily resolve.

When you combine these two, you see that mass human behaviour is rarely random. People are acting on deep-seated cultural programming (Anthropology) while being pushed and pulled by social structures, media, and psychological biases (Sociology & Social Psychology).

Enabling factors in transitions
A number of conditions appear to enable successful transitions:

- Economic security - surplus resources, no debt, stable income, own home, low commitments, multiple-income household
- Emotional security - supportive partner, stable childhood, support networks, openness on emotional and mental health issues
- Health - good physical fitness, prudent lifestyle, quality time for leisure.
- Prior transition skills - positive transition experiences, clear goals
- Supportive work environment - high respect / low control culture, good team morale, clear role and contract terms, life~work boundaries respected
- Transition support - briefing, monitoring issues, practical support, life~career planning, tolerance, dignity, valuing the past, time off before illness, confidential counselling, freedom/recognition for new ideas

Positive outcomes: minimise severity of distress in the crisis phase, minimise risks of quitting or extended crisis, optimising recovery time, high innovation, personal transformation, healing old wounds, ‘rejuvenated’ staff, high group morale and synergy, enable organisational transformation.


# Communication

Humans are uniquely cooperative, and communication is the invisible glue that holds our societies together.
- Beyond Words: Only a fraction of our communication is verbal. We rely heavily on non-verbal cues (body language, micro-expressions) and paralanguage (tone, pitch, pacing) to determine if someone is trustworthy.
- Shared Intentionality: Humans have a unique ability to collaborate toward a common goal because we can understand what another person is thinking and feeling (Theory of Mind). We don't just communicate data; we communicate shared realities.Minding your attitude
- focussed expectation, let go of judegements, blame or negative thoughts. You are now ready for positivity. People are drawn to positive attitudes. 

Engaging with your eyes
- eye contact indicates confidence, trust
Know what you want to say before you open your mouth, then make sure you can be understood.
- concentrate on your consonants to make sure you communicate what you mean
Whajado today?""What did you do today?"The tongue taps the roof of the mouth sharply for each D."I wanna go to the store.""I want to go to the store."The crisp NT and T provide rhythmic punctuation."Gimme a sec.""Give me a second."The biting V and final ND blend prevent slurring.

- highlight key words
- speak slowly enough to be understood
- lacing your sentences with appropriate words 
- avoid overloading your message with too many point
Listening with willingness
- 'Listening is the willingness to change'
- Powerful people need to be put in environments where they aren't as powerful to change

Letting go of negatvitiy


Feeding back what you heard
1. Paraphrase what you hear
2. Reflect the feeling behind the message
3. Summarise the speaker's main ideas and feelings to show understanding


Treat the other person with respect, politeness and kindness. How do you do this?
- encourage people to express their ideas and opinions
- letting others finish making their points before interjecting yours
- never insult, disparage or put down people or their points of view
- praise more frequently than you critise, and when you do criticise, focusing o the behaviour and not the person
- treat people the way they want to be treated

Preferred form of communication
- some people like to talk while they think and others prefer silence as they organise their thoughts
- some people like to focus on fine details and practical applications, others prefer concepts
- some people like the written word while others prefer phone or face to face

Thinking before you speak
- speak only if your words are: accurate, appropriate, effective, necessary, timely

Talking less and listening more
- 2 ears and 1 mouth
- confirm understanding by rephrasing what soemone says, and avoid interuptions.

Asking questions to understand
- be interested in what they say
- ask questions to gain information - the more you ask the more you find out

Handling disagreements with diplomacy
- come from different points of view
- handled well, conflict can clear the air and revitalise a relationship
- how to fix?
    - reflect your understanding of the other person's view or opinion. What I am hearing is ...
    - you respect the other person's opinion: I understand how you feel...
    - State your position or opinion - you value their relationship not creating conflict

Opening yourself to new ideas
You have been raised with a set of beliefs and values. The same is true for others. That doesn't mean you are right and they're wrong; you're just different. Open yourself to other points of view:
- make more informed decisions
- increase your personal creativity
- develop certainty, clarity on your own positions

Unconsidered idea lacks justification. When you consider new ides you may discover solid reasons for believing in what you do. Think from another point of view.
