Economics assumes humans act rationally; sociology proves we rarely do. This topic looks at how group dynamics, class structures, media consumption, and cognitive biases influence mass human behaviour and societal trends.

Anthropology - To understand global conflicts and alliances, you must understand what people value. Studying major world religions, cultural frameworks, and belief systems explains the underlying worldviews of different populations.

This lens looks at how you are shaped by the structures around you and how large groups of people behave collectively.Group Dynamics & Tribalism: Humans have an evolutionary need to belong. We naturally divide the world into "In-Groups" (people like us) and "Out-Groups" (the "others"). 

This creates intense loyalty to sports teams, political parties, or nations, often leading to automatic hostility toward outsiders.Class Structures: Every society stratifies people by wealth, education, or family lineage. Your social class largely dictates your access to power, your healthcare, your career path, and even how you view your own potential. It is the invisible ladder (or barrier) in daily life.Media 

Consumption: The information we consume acts as our window to reality. Today, algorithms feed us content that triggers high emotion (usually anger or validation). This creates "echo chambers," where massive groups of people develop entirely different versions of what they believe is "the truth," driving political polarization.Cognitive Biases: Our brains use mental shortcuts to process information quickly, but they distort reality. For example:Confirmation Bias: We only look for information that proves we are already right and ignore data that proves us wrong.Bandwagon Effect: We adopt beliefs simply because a lot of other people in our social group hold them.


What People Value: Western societies heavily value individual liberty, personal achievement, and freedom of speech. Many Eastern or Indigenous cultures place a much higher value on collective harmony, family duty, and respect for authority. Global conflicts often happen because one side tries to force its values onto a culture that operates on a completely different moral foundation.Major World Religions: Religion is not just about personal faith; it is a foundational blueprint for law, morality, and identity. It dictates what millions of people consider clean or unclean, sacred or disposable, and right or wrong. You cannot understand Middle Eastern geopolitics without understanding Islam, nor can you understand US political history without understanding Christian currents.Cultural Frameworks & Belief Systems: This is the lens through which a population views time, progress, and justice. For instance, some cultures view time as linear (always moving forward toward progress), while others view it as cyclical (history repeating itself). Some cultures value face-saving and honor above all else, meaning a public insult can spark a conflict that logic cannot easily resolve.

When you combine these two, you see that mass human behaviour is rarely random. People are acting on deep-seated cultural programming (Anthropology) while being pushed and pulled by social structures, media, and psychological biases (Sociology & Social Psychology).

# Thinking
Most people don't think. Including me.

actively manipulating, analyzing, evaluating, and synthesizing information to solve complex problems or create new understandings

Analyze: Draw connections among ideas and break information into parts (e.g., compare, organize).Evaluate: Justify a stand or decision, or judge the value of material (e.g., critique, assess).Create: Produce new or original work by assembling elements into a new pattern (e.g., design, construct)

Conceptual: The interrelationships among basic elements (principles, theories).Procedural: How to do something, methods, and criteria (algorithms, techniques).

1. Apply: Put Knowledge into ContextDo not just read about a concept; immediately use it in a real-world scenario.
Practice active problem-solving: Instead of just reading a guide, try to solve a problem or complete a task using the new information without looking at the steps.Create scenarios: Ask yourself, "How would an expert use this tool or concept to solve a real-world issue today?"Teach someone else: Explain the concept to a peer or write a simplified summary. Teaching forces you to operationalise what you know.

2. Analyse: Break It Apart. Deconstruct the information to understand how its components relate to one another.Compare and contrast: Take two competing theories, methods, or pieces of software and map out their similarities and differences.Mind mapping: Draw visual diagrams showing the cause-and-effect relationships between different concepts.Question the structure: Ask yourself, "What are the underlying assumptions here? What evidence supports this specific part of the argument?"

3. Evaluate: Critically Judge the Material
Formulate your own evidence-based judgements rather than accepting information at face value.Play devil's advocate: Intentionally argue against the author’s or instructor's point of view. Find the weak points in their logic. Assess the quality: Look at the data or sources provided. Ask, "Is this data current as of 2026? Is there a bias, or is a critical perspective missing?"Prioritise: If you are looking at multiple solutions to a problem, rank them based on efficiency, cost, or ethics, and justify your top choice.

4. Create: Build Something NewCombine what you have learned with your existing knowledge to construct a unique output.Design a project: If you are learning a new language, programming framework, or business strategy, build a small personal project from scratch.Hypothesise: Ask "What if?" questions. For example, "What would happen to this system if X variable completely changed?"Develop a framework: Create your own checklist, cheat sheet, or workflow model based on your new synthesis of the topic.


1. Slow Down the "Automatic" BrainOur brains are evolutionary hardwired to save energy. They prefer fast, emotional, and easy answers (what psychologists call System 1 thinking). To think deeply, you have to force your brain into System 2 (slow, logical, effortful thinking).
The 10-Second Pause: When you read a headline, get an email, or feel an impulse to speak, force a physical pause. Ask yourself: "Is my immediate reaction based on objective facts, or an emotional habit?"Label your thoughts: Instead of just experiencing an thought, observe it. Say to yourself, "I am having the thought that this task is too hard," rather than "This task is too hard." This detaches you from the autopilot.

2. Shift from "What" to "How" and "Why"Passive thinkers absorb what happened. Active thinkers investigate the mechanics behind it. Shift your internal commentary by adopting the Three Question Rule for everything you encounter:"What is the underlying assumption here?" (What is being taken for granted without proof?)"What is the counter-evidence?" (What would prove this idea completely wrong?)"What are the long-term consequences?" (If I do this, what happens next? And what happens after that?)3. Use Mental Frameworks to Structure Your MindThinking without a structure is just daydreaming or worrying. Use these established frameworks to guide your brain:  [ First Principles ]          [ Inversion ]             [ Second-Order ]
  Break a problem down      Figure out how to fail     Think about the ripple
  to its absolute truths     to discover how to win     effects of a decision

- First Principles (Deconstruction): Strip a problem down to its absolute, undeniable truths, and build a solution from there. Instead of saying, "I want to start a business but it costs too much money," ask, "What does a business actually need to start? A customer and a product. Do I need money to talk to a customer?"Inversion (Reverse Thinking): Instead of thinking, "How do I have a productive day?" ask, "How could I completely ruin my day?" Write down the answers (staying up late, checking social media first thing, eating junk food) and then intentionally avoid them.Second-Order Thinking: Ask yourself, "And then what?" If you make a choice, look past the immediate reward to the second and third waves of consequences.4. Create "Thinking Spaces"You cannot think if your brain is constantly being fed external stimulation. If you look at your phone every time you are bored, you are outsourcing your thought process to algorithms.Boredom is the catalyst: Let yourself experience empty time. Walk without headphones. Sit in a chair for 10 minutes without a screen, a book, or a distraction.Write it out: The human brain is a terrible holding pen but a fantastic processing engine. You do not know what you truly think until you try to write it down. If your thoughts feel muddy, open a blank document or notebook and write continuously for 10 minutes. The act of shaping thoughts into language forces clarity.
