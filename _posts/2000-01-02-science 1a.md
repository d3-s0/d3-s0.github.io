It's important that you share your results with fellow scientists so that they can learn from your work, critique it, and build upon it.  That's how the collective scientific knowledge grows. That's where a scientific lab report comes in. 
<img src="/imgs/science/science_write.PNG" alt="Migration" style="width: 100%; height: auto; display: block">

The structure of reports is:
1. [Introduction](#introduction)
2. [Methods](#methods)
3. [Results](#results)
4. [Evaluation](#evaluation)
5. [Conclusion](#conclusion)
6. [Further work](#further-work)

### Introduction
- Begin with a statement of the problem. The overall situation in industry. 
- What was the purpose of this study? 
- Then move to a narrow focus on what you are going to talk about in the body of the report.
- It may be worthwhile to write the introduction last if you are unsure how to frame the work.
- What was it previously based on?
- Was there a gap in our knowledge?
- Would doing this work fill that gap?
- Common benefits include: economics, speed, efficiency, health. We want to motivate the reader to keep reading the entire paper.
- What is the theoretical underpinning content? 
- Cite literature that supports the claim.
- End the introduction with a statement of aims. Do not say In this experiment; instead, be concise and direct: In order to determine X, Y and Z will be measured and compared


### Methods
- Always explain your methods in full; do not say according to manufacturer’s instructions. - Keep track of the methods you used for the experiment or project – use a log book or slides. - There should be enough detail to reproduce the results.
- Be concise; you do not need to state every small thing (e.g.\ every temperature and number) in the text – the data can show it. 
- The text of results should describe results in a way that brings the reader to key findings.

### Results
- Present findings with the principle of objectivity.
- Data should be separated from interpretation.
- Explain what the graph shows, not what the graph means.
- Results should be in chronological order so the reader can follow the process.
- Steps should be linked naturally – make it easy for the reader to follow.
- Include information that is useful; remove what is meaningless.
- Tone should be short, direct, and mostly past tense.
- Long sentences with many concepts will confuse the reader. Do not be afraid to repeat.
- Avoid using it, they, them; these words can be ambiguous.
We need to convey quantitative data:
- Guide the reader to break from the text, examine the images, then resume reading. This - transition must be smooth.
- The figure needs to allow the reader to understand what procedure was carried out and what kind of output you have chosen to show.
- They should not have to refer to the main body of text to understand it – use a detailed caption.

### Evaluation
The real flair with scientific writing comes from the critical analysis. Your:
- Ability to analyse the results
- Ability to arrive at conclusions that fit the data
- Ability to think about any caveats that should be applied at the discussion stage

 
**Background research**
- When reading other scientists work, think critically about the experiment. Do conclusions make sense? Does data conclusively prove the hypothesis, or are there other ways of interpreting the data? Can results be repeated? Are the sources of information reliable? You should ask if the group conducting the experiment was unbiased. 
- Who wrote it? 
- Are they an authority in the field?
- When and where did they write it?
- What did the authors want to find out?
- How did they do their investigation?
- What did they find?
- Do you believe them?
- Write your own notes on the topic – imagine you are talking to the author and telling them what you understand of the writing.
- Use your scientific rigour and poke holes in logic. Imagine you are a competing scientist in the same field.
- You need to show you had a systematic approach to finding papers.
- When evaluating papers, look for weaknesses in the design of the experiment and the results.
- Design of experiment:
  - Has the author made any unfounded assumptions?
  - Does the author misrepresent other pieces of research that you are aware of?
  - Are the pieces of research mentioned relevant and current?
  - Are there studies missing you expected to see referenced?
- Methods used:
  - Is sample size sufficient?
  - Was the duration of experiment sufficient to see a difference?
  - What were the control variables?
  - Are there any confounding factors not mentioned?

**Interpreting your own results**
- What results surprised you? What was expected? What makes you think this?
- What parallels and contrasts exist between your findings and those published in the literature?
- Bring the reader's attention back to the issues you raise (why it is important), and show how your findings contribute to that academic discussion.
- Include any shortcomings of the study – this helps readers see all the reasons contributing towards the findings.
- Remind the reader which ways worked vs not at all.
- Remind the reader which other things affected performance.
- Make a case for which consideration is most important (low cost, high effectiveness).
- Use this to justify the treatment you think is best, and would recommend.
- Have they fully answered the original question stated in the introduction?
- Do the data and conclusions support each other?
- Trends in the data.
- Exceptions to the trend.
- Differences in values between different subjects.
- Any statistical analysis?
  - If the experiment was done on a small sample, then mention it is a preliminary or pilot study.
  - Extrapolation should be treated with caution – can you be sure?

### Conclusion
- Remind the reader what the essay is about – the original problem.
- Remind them what method was used and how the best option was chosen.
- Connect to wider work. Relating findings to theory is very important.
- Cite any wider study you connect to.

#### Further work
- The results support the hypothesis that XXX though further studies must be done to prove or disprove this correlation beyond doubt.
- Definite claims are bad, such as The results show that\ldots.
- Do not say In this essay, I have
- Acknowledge complexity of the issue with mention of alternative scenarios.
- Usually, the next step is to recommend further work, but go beyond this basic level.
- Be specific: what type of work is needed? Would a larger sample help statistical interpretation? 
- Would a better control be useful? Do two designs need adjusting to be compatible?
