Scientific writing is fundamentally based on the scientific method, which says  every question is answerable, and the answers are independent of the author. The 
author is irrelevant; it is the knowledge and explanations themselves that are prized. As we ask questions and find answers, we add to the collective knowledge.

Scientific writing is easier than artistic English literature since it is written 
in a formulaic way. The real flair with scientific writing comes from the critical analysis. That is where you give your own thoughts about the topic – where you, the writer, present your conjecture to the reader.

Fellow scientists want to see critical skills in your:
- Ability to analyse the results
- Ability to arrive at conclusions that fit the data
- Ability to think about any caveats that should be applied at the discussion stage

Understand that the scientific method is self-correcting. As you experiment and find new knowledge, it replaces older understandings. We would not be able to be self-correcting if we were not willing to be critical of older knowledge. Being critical is not negative; it is checking that the calculation is reasonable and logical.

Think of the report as an hourglass in terms of scope. 
**Intro and discussion/conclusion are wide**
**Methods and results**
in the middle are narrow and focused.

*An introduction of ~1 page. A ~1 page related work section with a good density of citations - not too sparse but not too crowded. A well-designed pull figure (on page 1 or 2) and system figure (on page 3) that were not made in MS Paint. A technical section with some math symbols somewhere, results tables with lots of numbers and some of them bold, one additional cute analysis experiment, and the paper has exactly 8 pages (the page limit) and not a single line less.*

*Identify the core contribution. Before you start writing anything it’s important to identify the single core contribution that your paper makes to the field. I would especially highlight the word single.*

*I like to organize each of my paragraphs around a single concrete point stated on the first sentence that is then supported in the rest of the paragraph. This structure makes it easy for a reader to skim the paper. A good flow of ideas is then along the lines of 1) X (+define X if not obvious) is an important problem 2) The core challenges are this and that. 2) Previous work on X has addressed these with Y, but the problems with this are Z. 3) In this work we do W (?). 4) This has the following appealing properties and our experiments show this and that.*

*when you explain the model each section should: 1) explain clearly what is being done in the section, 2) explain what the core challenges are 3) explain what a baseline approach is or what others have done before 4) motivate and explain what you do 5) describe it.*

*Common mistake: the laundry list. One very common mistake to avoid is the “laundry list”, which looks as follows: “Here is the problem. Okay now to solve this problem first we do X, then we do Y, then we do Z, and now we do W, and here is what we get”. You should try very hard to avoid this structure. Each point should be justified, motivated, explained. Why do you do X or Y? What are the alternatives? What have others done? It’s okay to say things like this is common (add citation if possible). Your paper is not a report, an enumeration of what you’ve done, or some kind of a translation of your chronological notes and experiments into latex. It is a highly processed and very focused discussion of a problem, your approach and its context. It is supposed to teach your colleagues something and you have to justify your steps, not just describe what you did.*

*in your papers you never “study” or “investigate” (there are boring, passive, bad words); instead you “develop” or even better you “propose”. And you don’t present a “system” or, shudder, a “pipeline”; instead, you develop a “model”. You don’t learn “features”, you learn “representations”. And god forbid, you never “combine”, “modify” or “expand”. These are incremental, gross terms that will certainly get your paper rejected :).*

Observation:
    What have you observed? What is the problem?

Question:
    Question the observation – ask why or how.

Hypothesis:
    Make an educated guess about the cause of the problem.

Topic title:
    Once we have formed the hypothesis we need a topic title.
    Break down the problem into separate components: command, subject, and limit.

Example:
Compare and contrast the ways in which you can gain muscle in 1 year.
    Command: compare and contrast
    Subject: ways of gaining muscle
    Limit: in 1 year

## Introduction
Explain why what you are doing is worthwhile.
Begin with a statement of the problem.
Then outline what type of analysis you are going to do.
Then move to a narrow focus on what you are going to talk about in the body of the report.
It may be worthwhile to write the introduction last if you are unsure how to frame the work.
Questions:
What was it previously based on?
Was there a gap in our knowledge?
Would doing this work fill that gap?

Common benefits include: economics, speed, efficiency, health. We want to motivate the reader to keep reading the entire paper.

What is the theoretical underpinning content? Cite literature that supports the claim.
You will have to bring back the citations in the conclusion.
End the introduction with a statement of aims. Do not say In this experiment; instead, be concise and direct: In order to determine X, Y and Z will be measured and compared


## Research
Read widely on the topic – research papers and books.
Keep track of sources.
While reading ask yourself:

Who wrote it? Are they an authority in the field?
When and where did they write it?
What did the authors want to find out?
How did they do their investigation?
What did they find?
Do you believe them?

Write your own notes on the topic – imagine you are talking to the author and telling them what you understand of the writing.
Use your scientific rigour and poke holes in logic. Imagine you are a competing scientist in the same field.
You need to show you had a systematic approach to finding papers.

When evaluating papers, look for weaknesses in the design of the experiment and the results.

Design of experiment:
Has the author made any unfounded assumptions?
Does the author misrepresent other pieces of research that you are aware of?
Are the pieces of research mentioned relevant and current?
Are there studies missing you expected to see referenced?

Methods used:
Is sample size sufficient?
Was the duration of experiment sufficient to see a difference?
What were the control variables?
Are there any confounding factors not mentioned?

## Methods
Always explain your methods in full; do not say according to manufacturer’s instructions. Keep track of the methods you used for the experiment or project – use a log book or slides. There should be enough detail to reproduce the results.
Be concise; you do not need to state every small thing (e.g.\ every temperature and number) in the text – the data can show it. The text of results should describe results in a way that brings the reader to key findings.

## Present your findings
Present findings with the principle of objectivity.
Data should be separated from interpretation.
Explain what the graph shows, not what the graph means.
Results should be in chronological order so the reader can follow the process.
Steps should be linked naturally – make it easy for the reader to follow.
Include information that is useful; remove what is meaningless.
Tone should be short, direct, and mostly past tense.
Long sentences with many concepts will confuse the reader. Do not be afraid to repeat.
Avoid using it, they, them; these words can be ambiguous.

We need to convey quantitative data:
Guide the reader to break from the text, examine the images, then resume reading. This transition must be smooth.
The figure needs to allow the reader to understand what procedure was carried out and what kind of output you have chosen to show.
They should not have to refer to the main body of text to understand it – use a detailed caption.


## Analyse your results

This is where you interpret the results:
What results surprised you? What was expected? What makes you think this?
What parallels and contrasts exist between your findings and those published in the literature?


Bring the reader's attention back to the issues you raise (why it is important), and show how your findings contribute to that academic discussion.
Include any shortcomings of the study – this helps readers see all the reasons contributing towards the findings.
Remind the reader which ways worked vs not at all.
Remind the reader which other things affected performance.
Make a case for which consideration is most important (low cost, high effectiveness).
Use this to justify the treatment you think is best, and would recommend.
When reading the discussion, check:
Have they fully answered the original question stated in the introduction?
Do the data and conclusions support each other?
Trends in the data.
Exceptions to the trend.
Differences in values between different subjects.
Any statistical analysis?
If the experiment was done on a small sample, then mention it is a preliminary or pilot study.
Extrapolation should be treated with caution – can you be sure?

## Conclusion
Remind the reader what the essay is about – the original problem.
Remind them what method was used and how the best option was chosen.
Connect to wider work. Relating findings to theory is very important.
Cite any wider study you connect to.

## Further work
Example sentence for further testing:
The results support the hypothesis that\ldots\ though further studies must be done to prove or disprove this correlation beyond doubt.
Definite claims are bad, such as The results show that\ldots.
Do not say In this essay, I have\ldots.
Acknowledge complexity of the issue with mention of alternative scenarios.
Usually, the next step is to recommend further work, but go beyond this basic level.
Be specific: what type of work is needed? Would a larger sample help statistical interpretation? Would a better control be useful? Do two designs need adjusting to be compatible?
