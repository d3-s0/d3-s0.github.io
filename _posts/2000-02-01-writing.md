## How do I start writing?
1. **What** do you want to write? 
    - Research what you want to write about
    - Now you need to inform a target audience about this
2. **Why** do you want to write it?
    - Why should someone read it?
3. **Who** are you writing for?
    - Write out who is the target audience for this text.
    - Does your audience know everything you know? Think from the stance of the reader.
    - Does the audience feel the same way you do?
    - Does the audience expect something specific from you?

## Arguments
Argumentative writing takes a position and supports it with reason and evidence. An argument consists of:
- introduction: introduce the problem
- claim: the point you want to prove
- evidence: facts that support argument
- reason: logic that connects evidence to argument
- conclusions: summary of argument or final judgement

Organisation helps you structure your writing so it's as persuasive as possible. In an argument, the order matters a lot. If you start giving evidence before you state your claim, people may not know what you're trying to prove. If you state your reasons before you give your evidence, people may thing you ar making it up. Everything needs to be in the right order with your claims supported by evidence and reasons, and all of it leading up to your logical conclusions.

Transitions
- evidence: due to, specifically, for example, after all, for instance, to illustrate
- reasons: therefore, so, because, thus, in other words
- conclusion: as a result, hence, finally

## Narratives

A narrative is a description of events that are connected to each other - story. Fiction writers use narratives to describe imaginary events. Non-fiction use narratives to describe real events. 
Narratives contain these things:
- context/settings
- point of view / narration
- characters
- plot
- conclusion

**context and setting**
readers need both to orient themselves to a story.
Context:
- what else is happening at the same time as the story?
- what happened before the story begins that is important for the reader to know so he understands the story.
Setting
- when a story happens
- where a story happens

How do you create a setting?
- what does the setting look like?
- what does the setting sound like?
- what does it feel to be there?
- what is the historical time and place of the setting?
- what is going on in the world at that time?

**narration**
- Do I want my narrator's thoughts and feelings to be a major part of the story? (first person)
- Do I want readers to identify strongly with the reader? (second person)
- Do I want the freedom to explore different points of view? (third person)
- Do i want the narration to be objective or subjective?
- Would it be more interesting for the narrative to be untrustworthy?


**characters**
- who are the characters in the story?

**plot**
- where would it be most interesting for story to begin?
- where do i want story to end?
- what is the conflict?
- what change must happen in the character?
- what does the reader to know to understand what's happening?
- what would be good to keep hidden from the reader?
- when is the best time to reveal details you've hidden to build drama?

How do you develop characters or narrative?
- dialogue
- pacing
- description
    - imprecise word choice
    - precise word choice

**conclusion**
everyone wants to know what happens at the end. the reader wants a conclusion. 
- ending must be logical from the story
- characters should act as they normally would








# Grammar
Grammar is the structure of a language - not what words mean, but the way the words fit together. It’s the architect from which you build the sentences - once you have the frame ready you can write anything. 
- Noun:  person, place or thing e.g. book
- Verb: action, state of being, event e.g. write
- Adjective: describes noun e.g. blue
- Adverb: modifies or describes verb e.g quickly
- Preposition: words that show direction, location or time
- Clause = subject + verb
- Independent clause = can stand alone as as a sentence.
- Dependent clause = can't stand alone as a sentence. 
- Compound sentence= independent clause + coordinating conjunction  + independent  clause
- Complex sentence
- Complex compound sentence = 2 or more independent  clauses and one dependent clause
- Conjunction: connects clauses or sentences 
- Pronouns: takes place of a person, place or thing

**Active an passive voice**
- Verb can be active(subject does something) or passive
- Usually active voice is better
- Moods of verbs
    - Indicative verbs: for facts
    - Imperative verbs: for orders
    - Interrogative verbs: direct questions
    - Conditional verbs: if conditions 
    - Subjunctive verbs: for wishes, hypothetical

**Figurative language**
- Figures of speech
- Personification
- Allusion: refer to it without referring to it
- Verbal irony
- Puns

**Word relationships**
- Cause/effect 
- Synonyms and antonyms
- Analogy
