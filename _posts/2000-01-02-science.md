# Scientific method
Science is a way of finding answers to questions about the world. Scientists find evidence by doing experiments to solve problems. This process is called Scientific Inquiry. Trying to find the answers to questions is called the Scientific Method.

The inquiry begins with a question about the world. After the question is identified, the next step is to collect all that relates to that investigation by doing background research, making observations and conducting experiments. 

The prediction is called hypothesis, the hypothesis is tested by making observations and comparing them to predictions. Some observations are quantitative while others are qualitative. 

After a hypothesis has been confirmed through many tests and experiments, scientists can develop a theory. A theory is a proposed explanation that has been tested rigorously. 

0. Background research on a topic
1. Observation : Look at the world and spot a phenomenon.
2. Hypothesis : Formulate a logical explanation or prediction.
3. Experiment : Design a controlled test to isolate variables.
4. Data Analysis: Use mathematics to interpret the results.
5. Conclusion : Prove, disprove, or modify the original idea.
6. Peer Review : Share findings openly so others can test it too.

Scientific experiments should follow a procedure such that another scientist should be able to repeat the experiment based on your procedure steps alone. You can have a controlled experiment by running an experiment more than once, first without changing any factors: control then second changing only factor you want chaning .
In a control experiment, the factors held constant are called constants and they don't affect the outcome of the experiment. A variable is a factor that can alter your experiment results. 
- Independant variable is the variable you change in an experiment
- Dependant variable is the variable that is influenced by the independant variable

Did your results support your hypothesis? If not, how would you change hypothesis to fit your results? Sometimes conclusions aren't immediately apparent and you will have to infer, or use observations and facts, to reach a conclusion. 


The Scientific Revolution (1500-1700) when empirical observation and experimentation replaced religious beliefs. 

Francis Bacon championed empiricism (gaining knowledge through direct observation and inductive reasoning), while René Descartes promoted deductive reasoning and mathematical analysis. Together, they built the modern scientific method: asking questions, forming hypotheses, testing them through experiments, and peer review. 

This era weakened the intellectual monopoly of the Church, shifted the focus of human thought toward rationalism, and directly laid the groundwork for the Age of Enlightenment and the modern industrial world.



## Scientific Writing
It's important that you share your results with fellow scientists so that they can learn from your work, critque it, and build upon it. That's how the collective scientific knowledge grows. That's where a scientific lab report comes in. The structure of reports is:

1. [Introduce the problem](#introduction)
2. [Background](#research)
3. [Methods](#methods)
4. [Results](#results)
5. [Evaluation](#evaluation)
6. [Conclusion](#conclusion)
7. [Further work](#further-work)

### Introduction
Begin with a statement of the problem. The overall situation in industry. What was the purpose of this study? Then move to a narrow focus on what you are going to talk about in the body of the report.
It may be worthwhile to write the introduction last if you are unsure how to frame the work.
What was it previously based on?
Was there a gap in our knowledge?
Would doing this work fill that gap?

Common benefits include: economics, speed, efficiency, health. We want to motivate the reader to keep reading the entire paper.

What is the theoretical underpinning content? Cite literature that supports the claim.
You will have to bring back the citations in the conclusion.
End the introduction with a statement of aims. Do not say In this experiment; instead, be concise and direct: In order to determine X, Y and Z will be measured and compared


### Background
Read widely on the topic – research papers and books.
Keep track of sources.
While reading ask yourself:

Who wrote it? Are they an authority in the field?
When and where did they write it?
What did the authors want to find out?
How did they do their investigation?
What did they find?
Do you believe them?

Write your own notes on the topic – imagine you are talking to the author and telling them what you understand of the writing.
Use your scientific rigour and poke holes in logic. Imagine you are a competing scientist in the same field.
You need to show you had a systematic approach to finding papers.

When evaluating papers, look for weaknesses in the design of the experiment and the results.

Design of experiment:
Has the author made any unfounded assumptions?
Does the author misrepresent other pieces of research that you are aware of?
Are the pieces of research mentioned relevant and current?
Are there studies missing you expected to see referenced?

Methods used:
Is sample size sufficient?
Was the duration of experiment sufficient to see a difference?
What were the control variables?
Are there any confounding factors not mentioned?

### Methods
Always explain your methods in full; do not say according to manufacturer’s instructions. Keep track of the methods you used for the experiment or project – use a log book or slides. There should be enough detail to reproduce the results.
Be concise; you do not need to state every small thing (e.g.\ every temperature and number) in the text – the data can show it. The text of results should describe results in a way that brings the reader to key findings.

### Results
Present findings with the principle of objectivity.
Data should be separated from interpretation.
Explain what the graph shows, not what the graph means.
Results should be in chronological order so the reader can follow the process.
Steps should be linked naturally – make it easy for the reader to follow.
Include information that is useful; remove what is meaningless.
Tone should be short, direct, and mostly past tense.
Long sentences with many concepts will confuse the reader. Do not be afraid to repeat.
Avoid using it, they, them; these words can be ambiguous.

We need to convey quantitative data:
Guide the reader to break from the text, examine the images, then resume reading. This transition must be smooth.
The figure needs to allow the reader to understand what procedure was carried out and what kind of output you have chosen to show.
They should not have to refer to the main body of text to understand it – use a detailed caption.


### Evaluation
The real flair with scientific writing comes from the critical analysis. Your:
- Ability to analyse the results
- Ability to arrive at conclusions that fit the data
- Ability to think about any caveats that should be applied at the discussion stage

When reading other scientists work, think critically about the experiment. Do conclusions make sense? Does data conclusively prove the hypothesis, or are there other ways of interpreting the data? Can results be repeated? Are the sources of information reliable? You should ask if the group conducting the experiment was unbiased. 
 
This is where you interpret the results:
What results surprised you? What was expected? What makes you think this?
What parallels and contrasts exist between your findings and those published in the literature?
Bring the reader's attention back to the issues you raise (why it is important), and show how your findings contribute to that academic discussion.
Include any shortcomings of the study – this helps readers see all the reasons contributing towards the findings.
Remind the reader which ways worked vs not at all.
Remind the reader which other things affected performance.
Make a case for which consideration is most important (low cost, high effectiveness).
Use this to justify the treatment you think is best, and would recommend.
When reading the discussion, check:
Have they fully answered the original question stated in the introduction?
Do the data and conclusions support each other?
Trends in the data.
Exceptions to the trend.
Differences in values between different subjects.
Any statistical analysis?
If the experiment was done on a small sample, then mention it is a preliminary or pilot study.
Extrapolation should be treated with caution – can you be sure?

### Conclusion
Remind the reader what the essay is about – the original problem.
Remind them what method was used and how the best option was chosen.
Connect to wider work. Relating findings to theory is very important.
Cite any wider study you connect to.

### Further work
Example sentence for further testing:
The results support the hypothesis that\ldots\ though further studies must be done to prove or disprove this correlation beyond doubt.
Definite claims are bad, such as The results show that\ldots.
Do not say In this essay, I have\ldots.
Acknowledge complexity of the issue with mention of alternative scenarios.
Usually, the next step is to recommend further work, but go beyond this basic level.
Be specific: what type of work is needed? Would a larger sample help statistical interpretation? Would a better control be useful? Do two designs need adjusting to be compatible?

## Relativity
#### Special relativity
Special relativity is the rule that the speed of light never changes, which means that space and time must stretch or shrink depending on how fast you are moving

#### General relativity (mass and gravity)
General relativity is the rule that mass and energy warp space and time, which is what creates the effect we feel as gravity.

Instead of gravity being an invisible pulling force, imagine space and time as a giant trampoline. A heavy object like the Earth makes a dip in the fabric, and smaller objects like the Moon or a falling apple just roll down that dip.

## Quantum physics
Quantum physics is the study of the universe at the smallest possible scale—atoms, electrons, and photons. At this level, nature stops acting like predictable, continuous billard balls and starts acting like fuzzy, overlapping waves of probability.

Quantisation: Energy is not a smooth ramp; it is a staircase. Matter and light can only absorb or release energy in tiny, discrete packets called quanta.

Wave-Particle Duality: Everything travels like a wave but interacts like a particle. An electron can ripple through space like water, but hits a detector at one precise point.


# Earth science

To understand how the earth is warming up, let's turn to the Earth's atmosphere. 
Our atmosphere protects us from harmful radiation from the Sun and also keeps us warm enough for life to exist, it does let in shortwave solar radiation, which hits the Earth's surface and heats it up. Earth then emits infrared radiation which is then absorbed by the gases in the air. This is known as the greenhouse effect. The gases that absorb the energy are called greenhouse gases. These are:
- Carbon dioxide (Co2)
- Water vapour (H20)
- Nitrous oxide (N20)
- Ozone (O3)
- Methane (CH4)
- Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and other fluorinated gases

## Fossil fuel burning
The three fossil fuels are coal, oil and gas. Coal is made from dead land plants being compacted over millions of years. Oil and gas are hydrocarbons that are made from marine plankton in the shallow sea floor that like carbon has been compacted over millions of years. Here are the the combustion equations for them:
- Methane: CH4+2O2→CO2+2H2O
- Coal: C+O2→CO2
- Oil: E.g. Diesel: 4C12H23+71O2→48CO2+46H2O

From the above equations, we can see that burning the fossil fuel results in CO2 in all cases. If we didn't burn the fossil fuel, the CO2 would remain in the ground and recycle over millions of years but by us injecting the CO2 directly into the atmosphere, we are releasing more carbon than the Earth can absorb the CO2 hence CO2 levels increase.

## Deforestation
Deforestation is when we destroy forests to make of them for agriculture or urbanisation. When we clear these forests we are removing natural carbon sinks. A carbon sink is a natural reservoir of carbon that absorbs more carbon than it emits. Furthermore, once we clear the forest we may use the wood for burning which also releases the carbon hence increasing the CO2 levels further. Even replanting trees takes a very long time to absorb the amount of carbon the older trees released as younger trees can't absorb as much CO2.

### Power stations
1. Fuel supply: Coal is crushed and fed into a large furnace to be burned. 
2. Furnace: Water running through tubes in the boiler is heated to an over 500C at very high pressure. Waste gasses pass through an electrostatic precipitator and desulphurisation plant to clean.
3. Turbine: the high pressure steam turns the fan blades of steam turbines with great force and speed. This rotational motion is transmitted to the generator by driveshaft.
4. Generator: generator's rotor spins thousands of times each minute, producing an alternating current (AC), typically with a voltage of 25,000V. 
5. Electricity supply: voltage of electricity is greatly increased by a step-up transformer. This improves efficiency as the electricity is transmitted away via power lines.
6. Cooling tower: steam is cooled in the condenser then sprayed into cooling towers, where most of the water cools and is piped back for re-use. 

### Electricity supply
1. Power station: a generator located in a power station converts kinetic energy into electrical energy. This produced AC electricity - 25,000V
2. Grid substation: A grid substation uses step up transformer to increase voltage to 400,000V. The higher the voltage the less energy lost as heat from resistance.
3. High voltage pylon lines: steel-reinforced aluminum pylons made with glass/ceramic insulators to stop electricity traveling to the ground.
4. Direct supply to industry: some factories want high voltage!
5. Distribution substation: a step down transformer used to bring voltage down -> industrial and commercial customers.
6. Domestic supply: a network of distribution lines supplies electricity to houses. pole mounted transformers locally bring the voltage down to the home's fusebox.

### Nuclear power
1. Uranium fuel rods: Hundreds of metal rods grouped into bundles contain small pellets of uranium fuel. They are lowered into reactor core.
2. Chain reaction: Unstable uranium nuclei break down, releasing heat and emitting neutrons. These collide with further nuclei, creating a chain reaction that provides a huge amounts of energy.
3. Control rods: control rods modify the speed of chain reaction. When lowered in among the fuel rods they absorb the free neutrons to slow down the reaction.
4. Creating steam: heated by the reactor core, the water flows into a heat exchanger, giving up its energy to a second closed system of piping that carries cold water. The cold water is turned into hot steam, under high pressure.
5. Turning the turbine: housed in a turbine hall, the turbine's fan blades are turned by the high-pressure steam from the heat exchanger. 
6. Power supply: the electricity generator is driven by the turbine's drive shaft. A transformer increases the voltage so it can be carried away.

#### CPU
The CPU is the brain of the computer, responsible for general-purpose computing. It contains billions of transistors, which act as tiny switches representing binary data (1 and 0). The CPU performs tasks in multiple steps: 
1.	fetching - control unit receives instruction from memory
2.	decoding - control interprets the instruction

Sand to transistor
But what is this transistor?
A transistor is a three-terminal semiconductor device with the collector, base, and emitter. By applying a small current to the base, a larger current flows between collector and emitter. This switching represents binary states. In a modern CPU, binary logic is primarily formed using MOSFETs (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistors)
Transistors act as microscopic, high-speed switches that represent binary states (1 for "on" and 0 for "off"). By combining several transistors, engineers create Logic Gates, which perform basic mathematical and logical operations. NOT Gate: Inverts the signal (1 becomes 0). AND Gate: Outputs 1 only if both inputs are 1. OR Gate: Outputs 1 if at least one input is 1. NAND/NOR Gates: These "universal" gates are particularly efficient to build with MOSFETs and can be used to construct any other type of logic. 
How do you make a transistor? Sand.
The most common form of sand is silicon dioxide (SiO₂), found in quartz.
Silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth’s crust, and sand provides a cheap, plentiful raw source. Raw sand can’t be directly used for chips. It first goes through chemical reduction processes (like the carbothermic reduction in a furnace) to make metallurgical-grade silicon (~98–99% pure). That silicon is purified further via the Siemens process into extremely pure polysilicon (99% pure, or “9N” purity). This step is critical — impurities at parts per billion can make chips fail. Using the Czochralski process or float-zone refining, the pure polysilicon is grown into large, single-crystal silicon ingots. These ingots are sliced into thin wafers — the starting substrate for chips. On the wafer, layers of materials are deposited, patterned, and etched using processes like photolithography, doping, oxidation, and etching.
Billions of transistors are created on each wafer.

#### OS
Okay we have the hardware ready. How does it go into becoming the software?
1.	PSU powers the motherboard: The Power Supply Unit sends a steady electrical current across the motherboard circuits.
2.	BIOS/UEFI wakes up: This electricity activates a permanent flash memory chip containing the computer's very first software instructions.
3.	BIOS searches for the OS: The BIOS software looks for data indicating where the Operating System is installed.
4.	Motherboard reads storage: The motherboard scans your permanent storage drives (HDD or SSD) to locate this data.
5.	Bootloader is located: The motherboard finds a specific sector on the drive containing a tiny software program called the Bootloader.
6.	Bootloader copies the OS: The Bootloader copies the heavy Operating System files from your slow permanent storage.
7.	Data loads into RAM: The Bootloader pastes these files directly into your high-speed, temporary Random Access Memory.
8.	CPU bypasses storage: The CPU uses the RAM as an immediate workspace because reading directly from an HDD or SSD is too slow.
9.	OS takes total control: The Operating System fully loads from RAM, activates, and launches your desktop user interface.
10.	CPU processes code: The CPU reads digital instructions from the RAM billions of times per second to run your desktop.
11.	GPU renders graphics: The CPU hands visual tasks to the Graphics Processing Unit to display images on your screen.
12.	System manages future apps: When you open a new app, the OS fetches it from the SSD, places it in RAM, and tells the CPU to execute it
