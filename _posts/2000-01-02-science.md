# Scientific method
Science is a way of finding answers to questions about the world. Scientists find evidence by doing experiments to solve problems. This process is called Scientific Inquiry. Trying to find the answers to questions is called the Scientific Method. The inquiry begins with a question about the world. After the question is identified, the next step is to collect all that relates to that investigation by doing background research, making observations and conducting experiments. The prediction is called hypothesis, the hypothesis is tested by making observations and comparing them to predictions. Some observations are quantitative while others are qualitative. After a hypothesis has been confirmed through many tests and experiments, scientists can develop a theory. A theory is a proposed explanation that has been tested rigorously. 

1. Observation : Look at the world and spot a phenomenon.
2. Hypothesis : Formulate a logical explanation or prediction.
3. Experiment : Design a controlled test to isolate variables.
4. Data Analysis: Use mathematics to interpret the results.
5. Conclusion : Prove, disprove, or modify the original idea.
6. Peer Review : Share findings openly so others can test it too.

Scientific experiments should follow a procedure such that another scientist should be able to repeat the experiment based on your procedure steps alone. You can have a controlled experiment by running an experiment more than once, first without changing any factors: control then second changing only factor you want changing.

In a control experiment, the factors held constant are called constants and they don't affect the outcome of the experiment. A variable is a factor that can alter your experiment results. 
- Independent variable is the variable you change in an experiment
- Dependant variable is the variable that is influenced by the independent variable
Did your results support your hypothesis? If not, how would you change hypothesis to fit your results? Sometimes conclusions aren't immediately apparent and you will have to infer, or use observations and facts, to reach a conclusion. 

The Scientific Revolution (1500-1700) when empirical observation and experimentation replaced religious beliefs. Francis Bacon championed empiricism (gaining knowledge through direct observation and inductive reasoning), while Rene Descartes promoted deductive reasoning and mathematical analysis. Together, they built the modern scientific method: asking questions, forming hypotheses, testing them through experiments, and peer review.  This era weakened the intellectual monopoly of the Church, shifted the focus of human thought toward rationalism, and directly laid the groundwork for the Age of Enlightenment and the modern industrial world.


## Scientific Writing
It's important that you share your results with fellow scientists so that they can learn from your work, critique it, and build upon it. That's how the collective scientific knowledge grows. That's where a scientific lab report comes in. The structure of reports is:

1. [Introduction](#introduction)
2. [Methods](#methods)
3. [Results](#results)
4. [Evaluation](#evaluation)
5. [Conclusion](#conclusion)
6. [Further work](#further-work)

### Introduction
Begin with a statement of the problem. The overall situation in industry. What was the purpose of this study? Then move to a narrow focus on what you are going to talk about in the body of the report.
It may be worthwhile to write the introduction last if you are unsure how to frame the work.
What was it previously based on?
Was there a gap in our knowledge?
Would doing this work fill that gap?

Common benefits include: economics, speed, efficiency, health. We want to motivate the reader to keep reading the entire paper.

What is the theoretical underpinning content? Cite literature that supports the claim.
You will have to bring back the citations in the conclusion.
End the introduction with a statement of aims. Do not say In this experiment; instead, be concise and direct: In order to determine X, Y and Z will be measured and compared

Read widely on the topic – research papers and books.
Keep track of sources.
While reading ask yourself:

Who wrote it? Are they an authority in the field?
When and where did they write it?
What did the authors want to find out?
How did they do their investigation?
What did they find?
Do you believe them?

Write your own notes on the topic – imagine you are talking to the author and telling them what you understand of the writing.
Use your scientific rigour and poke holes in logic. Imagine you are a competing scientist in the same field.
You need to show you had a systematic approach to finding papers.

When evaluating papers, look for weaknesses in the design of the experiment and the results.

Design of experiment:
Has the author made any unfounded assumptions?
Does the author misrepresent other pieces of research that you are aware of?
Are the pieces of research mentioned relevant and current?
Are there studies missing you expected to see referenced?

Methods used:
Is sample size sufficient?
Was the duration of experiment sufficient to see a difference?
What were the control variables?
Are there any confounding factors not mentioned?

### Methods
Always explain your methods in full; do not say according to manufacturer’s instructions. Keep track of the methods you used for the experiment or project – use a log book or slides. There should be enough detail to reproduce the results.
Be concise; you do not need to state every small thing (e.g.\ every temperature and number) in the text – the data can show it. The text of results should describe results in a way that brings the reader to key findings.

### Results
Present findings with the principle of objectivity.
Data should be separated from interpretation.
Explain what the graph shows, not what the graph means.
Results should be in chronological order so the reader can follow the process.
Steps should be linked naturally – make it easy for the reader to follow.
Include information that is useful; remove what is meaningless.
Tone should be short, direct, and mostly past tense.
Long sentences with many concepts will confuse the reader. Do not be afraid to repeat.
Avoid using it, they, them; these words can be ambiguous.

We need to convey quantitative data:
Guide the reader to break from the text, examine the images, then resume reading. This transition must be smooth.
The figure needs to allow the reader to understand what procedure was carried out and what kind of output you have chosen to show.
They should not have to refer to the main body of text to understand it – use a detailed caption.


### Evaluation
The real flair with scientific writing comes from the critical analysis. Your:
- Ability to analyse the results
- Ability to arrive at conclusions that fit the data
- Ability to think about any caveats that should be applied at the discussion stage

When reading other scientists work, think critically about the experiment. Do conclusions make sense? Does data conclusively prove the hypothesis, or are there other ways of interpreting the data? Can results be repeated? Are the sources of information reliable? You should ask if the group conducting the experiment was unbiased. 
 
This is where you interpret the results:
What results surprised you? What was expected? What makes you think this?
What parallels and contrasts exist between your findings and those published in the literature?
Bring the reader's attention back to the issues you raise (why it is important), and show how your findings contribute to that academic discussion.
Include any shortcomings of the study – this helps readers see all the reasons contributing towards the findings.
Remind the reader which ways worked vs not at all.
Remind the reader which other things affected performance.
Make a case for which consideration is most important (low cost, high effectiveness).
Use this to justify the treatment you think is best, and would recommend.
When reading the discussion, check:
Have they fully answered the original question stated in the introduction?
Do the data and conclusions support each other?
Trends in the data.
Exceptions to the trend.
Differences in values between different subjects.
Any statistical analysis?
If the experiment was done on a small sample, then mention it is a preliminary or pilot study.
Extrapolation should be treated with caution – can you be sure?

### Conclusion
Remind the reader what the essay is about – the original problem.
Remind them what method was used and how the best option was chosen.
Connect to wider work. Relating findings to theory is very important.
Cite any wider study you connect to.

#### Further work
Example sentence for further testing:
The results support the hypothesis that\ldots\ though further studies must be done to prove or disprove this correlation beyond doubt.
Definite claims are bad, such as The results show that\ldots.
Do not say In this essay, I have\ldots.
Acknowledge complexity of the issue with mention of alternative scenarios.
Usually, the next step is to recommend further work, but go beyond this basic level.
Be specific: what type of work is needed? Would a larger sample help statistical interpretation? Would a better control be useful? Do two designs need adjusting to be compatible?

## Statistics
- Descriptive Statistics: Summarizing data using measures of central tendency (mean, median, mode) and dispersion (variance, standard deviation, interquartile range).
- Probability Theory: Understanding sample spaces, conditional probability, and Bayes' Theorem for classification and updating beliefs with new data.
- Probability Distributions: Recognizing common distributions such as Binomial, Poisson, Normal (Gaussian), and Uniform.
- Inferential Statistics: Using sample data to make inferences about a larger population through confidence intervals and p-values.
- Hypothesis Testing: Conducting Z-tests, T-tests, and Chi-Square tests to validate assumptions and measure significance.
- Modeling & Inference: Working with linear regression, generalized linear models (GLMs), maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), and the Central Limit Theore

## Probability
Probability measures how likely an event is to occur, ranging from 0 to 1.
Sample space: all possible outcomes.
Events: one or more outcomes from the sample space.
Basic rules: addition, multiplication, and complement laws.
Types of events: independent (no influence), mutually exclusive (cannot happen together).
Conditional probability and Bayes’ theorem for updating probabilities.
Counting and Combinatorics
Counting methods help calculate probabilities when many outcomes exist.
Permutations: arrangements where order matters.
Combinations: selections where order does not matter.
Types of Probability
Theoretical: Based on logical reasoning (e.g. a fair coin has 1⁄2 chance of heads).
Experimental (empirical): Based on data from experiments or past events.
Subjective: Based on opinion or belief (e.g. a fan says their team has an 80% chance to win).
Random Variables and Distributions
Random variables can be discrete or continuous.
Described using probability mass (pmf) or density (pdf) functions.
Common distributions: binomial, Poisson, uniform, exponential, and normal (Gaussian).
Key measures: mean, variance, and standard deviation.

## Relativity
#### Special relativity
Special relativity is the rule that the speed of light never changes, which means that space and time must stretch or shrink depending on how fast you are moving

#### General relativity (mass and gravity)
General relativity is the rule that mass and energy warp space and time, which is what creates the effect we feel as gravity.

Instead of gravity being an invisible pulling force, imagine space and time as a giant trampoline. A heavy object like the Earth makes a dip in the fabric, and smaller objects like the Moon or a falling apple just roll down that dip.

## Quantum physics
Quantum physics is the study of the universe at the smallest possible scale—atoms, electrons, and photons. At this level, nature stops acting like predictable, continuous billard balls and starts acting like fuzzy, overlapping waves of probability.

Quantisation: Energy is not a smooth ramp; it is a staircase. Matter and light can only absorb or release energy in tiny, discrete packets called quanta.

Wave-Particle Duality: Everything travels like a wave but interacts like a particle. An electron can ripple through space like water, but hits a detector at one precise point.

# Intro to BEM
Having worked in the BEM sector for a while, it's good for me to write some notes on it. What is Building Energy Modelling? Building Energy Modelling (BEM) is the simulation of a building’s energy using physics to estimate energy consumption.
Building energy modelling comes in 2 main forms: steady state vs dynamic simulation modelling. Steady state is typically a simple monthly calculation whereas dynamic simulation modelling is the more advanced form where there are heat balance calculations.

What are the benefits of BEM?
- Demonstrate compliance with building regulation (government)
- Stock modelling helps inform energy efficiency policies (government)
- Make informed decisions about energy performance (developers and owners)
- Identify the most effective energy conservation measures and technologies for a specific building (retrofitters)

# Totals and energy sector
The UK energy sector can be broken into four main pillars:
1. **Generation**: Power stations, wind farms, and solar parks generate electricity. For gas, this involves extracting domestic supply (mostly from the North Sea) and importing the rest via pipelines from Europe or shipments of Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG)
2. **Transmission**: electricity cables and high-pressure gas pipes move large volumes of energy across long distances. This network is owned and maintained by monopolies, such as the National Grid 
3. **Distribution**: Regional networks take the energy from the high voltage transmission grid, step down the voltage or pressure at substations, and deliver it via regional networks (Distribution Network Operators or DNOs)
4. **Retail**: Energy suppliers (like British Gas, Octopus Energy, or E.ON) do not own the pipes and wires. Instead, they buy electricity and gas on the wholesale market and sell it to consumers

Marginal pricing
While renewables (primarily wind) generate a massive share of power, natural gas remains highly critical. Under the UK's marginal pricing system, the wholesale price of electricity is pinned to the cost of the final generator needed to meet demand—which is almost always gas. Thus global gas price spikes directly dictate UK electricity bills.

### Power stations
1. Fuel supply: Coal is crushed and fed into a large furnace to be burned. 
2. Furnace: Water running through tubes in the boiler is heated to an over 500C at very high pressure. Waste gasses pass through an electrostatic precipitator and desulphurisation plant to clean.
3. Turbine: the high pressure steam turns the fan blades of steam turbines with great force and speed. This rotational motion is transmitted to the generator by driveshaft.
4. Generator: generator's rotor spins thousands of times each minute, producing an alternating current (AC), typically with a voltage of 25,000V. 
5. Electricity supply: voltage of electricity is greatly increased by a step-up transformer. This improves efficiency as the electricity is transmitted away via power lines.
6. Cooling tower: steam is cooled in the condenser then sprayed into cooling towers, where most of the water cools and is piped back for re-use.

### Electricity supply
1. Power station: a generator located in a power station converts kinetic energy into electrical energy. This produced AC electricity - 25,000V
2. Grid substation: A grid substation uses step up transformer to increase voltage to 400,000V. The higher the voltage the less energy lost as heat from resistance.
3. High voltage pylon lines: steel-reinforced aluminum pylons made with glass/ceramic insulators to stop electricity traveling to the ground.
4. Direct supply to industry: some factories want high voltage!
5. Distribution substation: a step down transformer used to bring voltage down -> industrial and commercial customers.
6. Domestic supply: a network of distribution lines supplies electricity to houses. pole mounted transformers locally bring the voltage down to the home's fusebox.

1. Uranium fuel rods: Hundreds of metal rods grouped into bundles contain small pellets of uranium fuel. They are lowered into reactor core.
2. Chain reaction: Unstable uranium nuclei break down, releasing heat and emitting neutrons. These collide with further nuclei, creating a chain reaction that provides a huge amounts of energy.
3. Control rods: control rods modify the speed of chain reaction. When lowered in among the fuel rods they absorb the free neutrons to slow down the reaction.
4. Creating steam: heated by the reactor core, the water flows into a heat exchanger, giving up its energy to a second closed system of piping that carries cold water. The cold water is turned into hot steam, under high pressure.
5. Turning the turbine: housed in a turbine hall, the turbine's fan blades are turned by the high-pressure steam from the heat exchanger. 
6. Power supply: the electricity generator is driven by the turbine's drive shaft. A transformer increases the voltage so it can be carried away.

### Batteries
batteries are a portable store of chemical energy that can converted into electrical energy. Primary (single use) and secondary(rechargeable). 

1. chemical reactions: when a battery is connected to an electric circuit, a chemical reaction makes the metal atoms lose electrons. These are attracted to and gained by a chemical paste called electrolyte.
2. electrons gather: free electrons are drawn towards an anode, creating an imbalance with a deficit of electrons at the anode and an excess at the anode. The electrons freed by the chemical reaction move towards the anode inside the battery but cannot get any further until an eternal circuit is connected.
3. migrating electrons: the external circuit connecting the anode and cathode provide a path along which the electrons can flow, producing an electric current. Along the way, this current can be used to power an electrical device.
4. incoming electrons: the electrons re-enter the battery via the cathode. The flow continues until the store of chemicals is exhausted.

Recharging:
when a battery is plugged into a charger, a current is passed through in the opposite direction that was discharging. This moves electrons back to where they started, recharging the battery.

### How a car works
A car is a collection of systems that generates power in and engine and transmit it to the wheels. Other systems allow the driver to control the car by turning the wheels to change direction and by applying brake force to either slow down or stop.

1. engine: internal combustion engine - starting the engine ignites the fuel and releases energy. This moves pistons, which turns the engine's crankshaft. A flywheel attached to the crankshaft smooths out the power provided by the pistons
2. clutch: The driver must disconnect the clutch then releases clutch, allowing the engine to turn the wheel.
3. gearbox: piston engines work most efficiently at relatively high speeds, so gears are necessary to reduce this to a slower rotational speed needed for car's wheels. Each gear gives a different range of speeds. First gear is normally selected to get car moving.
4. driveshaft: a long driveshaft connects gearbox to rear wheels. In front wheel car, engine sits on top of the front wheels and turns them via short driveshaft, differential and half shafts.
5. steering: rack and pinion - turning car's steering wheel rotates a pinion - small round gear. it's teeth engage the teeth on a flat bar called a rack. 
5. braking: disc brakes - a disc is fixed to each wheel, when the wheel spins, so does the disc. When braking applied, hydraulic fluid forces brake pads, mounted on calipers to push against the disc to slow wheel down.

### how computers work
At the heart of every computer is an integrated circuit called a CPU. It communicates with the computer's main memory, input devices, and output devices. 


#### CPU
The CPU is the brain of the computer, responsible for general-purpose computing. It contains billions of transistors, which act as tiny switches representing binary data (1 and 0). The CPU performs tasks in multiple steps: 
1.	fetching - control unit receives instruction from memory
2.	decoding - control interprets the instruction
3.	execution - ALU performs calculation
4.	repeating 1-3
The CPU's speed, measured in gigahertz (GHz) indicates how many instructions the CPU can perform in a second.  More cores mean you can do multiple tasks at once. 
 
Sand to transistor
But what is this transistor?
A transistor is a three-terminal semiconductor device with the collector, base, and emitter. By applying a small current to the base, a larger current flows between collector and emitter. This switching represents binary states. In a modern CPU, binary logic is primarily formed using MOSFETs (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistors)
Transistors act as microscopic, high-speed switches that represent binary states (1 for "on" and 0 for "off"). By combining several transistors, engineers create Logic Gates, which perform basic mathematical and logical operations. NOT Gate: Inverts the signal (1 becomes 0). AND Gate: Outputs 1 only if both inputs are 1. OR Gate: Outputs 1 if at least one input is 1. NAND/NOR Gates: These "universal" gates are particularly efficient to build with MOSFETs and can be used to construct any other type of logic. 
How do you make a transistor? Sand.
The most common form of sand is silicon dioxide (SiO₂), found in quartz.
Silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth’s crust, and sand provides a cheap, plentiful raw source. Raw sand can’t be directly used for chips. It first goes through chemical reduction processes (like the carbothermic reduction in a furnace) to make metallurgical-grade silicon (~98–99% pure). That silicon is purified further via the Siemens process into extremely pure polysilicon (99% pure, or “9N” purity). This step is critical — impurities at parts per billion can make chips fail. Using the Czochralski process or float-zone refining, the pure polysilicon is grown into large, single-crystal silicon ingots. These ingots are sliced into thin wafers — the starting substrate for chips. On the wafer, layers of materials are deposited, patterned, and etched using processes like photolithography, doping, oxidation, and etching.
Billions of transistors are created on each wafer.

Categorical (Classification Models)
Classification models are used when the goal is to predict a discrete labelor category. For example, determining if an email is "Spam" or "Not Spam". 
Primary Models (Strictly for Classification):
Logistic Regression: Despite its name, it is a classification tool. It calculates the probability (0 to 1) that an input belongs to a specific class.
Naïve Bayes: Based on Bayes' Theorem, it assumes all input features are independent. It is exceptionally fast and often used for text and sentiment analysis.
Versatile Models (Used for Classification):
k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN): Classifies a new data point based on the majority label of its "K" closest neighbors in the dataset.
Support Vector Machines (SVM): Finds the best boundary (hyperplane) that separates different classes with the widest possible margin.
Decision Trees: A flow-chart-like structure where each "node" represents a choice based on data features, leading to a final classification.
Random Forest: An "ensemble" method that builds many decision trees and combines their results (majority vote) to improve accuracy.
Gradient Boosting: Builds trees sequentially, where each new tree specifically tries to correct the errors made by previous ones.
Neural Networks: Complex layers of "neurons" that mimic the human brain to find patterns in massive datasets, like recognizing faces in images. 
2. Continuous (Regression Models)
Regression models predict a numerical value rather than a category. For example, predicting a house's price might be 450,000. 
Primary Models (Strictly for Regression):
Linear Regression: Fits a straight line through data points to find the relationship between inputs and a continuous numerical output.
SVM Regression (SVR): A version of SVM that finds a boundary containing as many data points as possible within a specific error range.
Versatile Models (Used for Regression):
Decision Trees & Random Forest: Instead of a class label, the "leaf" of the tree provides the average value of the data points in that group.
kNN: Predicts a value by taking the average (mean) of the values of the "K" nearest neighbors.
Gradient Boosting: Sequentially adds trees to minimize the overall prediction error of numerical values.
Neural Networks: Use their complex layers to map inputs to a specific, real-world numerical output, such as weather forecasting or stock trends.

Gradient boosting
Gradient Boosting like a golf player trying to sink a hole-in-one.
The First Swing: You take a shot. It’s not great; the ball lands 50 yards away from the hole.
The Feedback: You don't start over. Instead, you look at the gap (the error) between the ball and the hole.
The Correction: You take a second, smaller swing specifically designed to cover that 50-yard gap.
Repeat: If you're still 5 yards off, your third swing is just a tiny tap to fix that specific mistake.
Gradient Boosting builds them one by one. Each new tree is specifically designed to correct the errors (called residuals) of the entire ensemble created so far. 
How the Algorithm Works (Step-by-Step)
Initialize the Model: Start with a simple "base" prediction, which is typically the mean of the target values for regression.
Calculate Residuals: Find the difference between the actual values and the current model's predictions.
Train a Weak Learner: Build a new decision tree that predicts these residuals instead of the original target.
Update Predictions: Add the new tree's predictions to the existing model, scaled by a learning rate (also known as "shrinkage") to prevent overfitting.
Repeat: Iterate through steps 2–4 for a set number of trees (e.g., 100 or 1,000).
